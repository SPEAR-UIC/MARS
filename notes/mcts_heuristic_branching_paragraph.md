# MCTS Heuristic Branching Draft

Each scheduling action at time $t$ can be viewed as an ordering of the jobs inside the head window $w$ of the waiting queue, which in principle yields $w!$ possible actions. While exhaustive enumeration is still feasible for very small windows, it becomes intractable once $w$ grows, even though prior work has shown that larger windows, often well beyond $w=20$, can produce meaningful scheduling gains \cite{fan_deep_2021, fan_scheduling_2019, zhang_rlscheduler_2020, yang-sc13}. In practice, however, many of these permutations are behaviorally redundant: under resource constraints and the FIFO+EASY semantics typical of HPC schedulers, different queue orderings often trigger the same set of job starts. This makes heuristic pruning a natural way to control the search space, as has also been observed in prior MCTS scheduling work \cite{mctsjobshop}. We therefore define MARS over a curated portfolio of heuristic-generated orderings rather than over all $w!$ permutations. Specifically, we score the jobs in the head window using each of the 16 non-FCFS heuristic policies in Table~\ref{tab:heuristic-policies}, sort the window accordingly, and pair each heuristic with window sizes $w \in \{2,4,8,\dots,1024\}$. Our preliminary heuristic-window study on Polaris further motivates this design: no single heuristic dominates across overall average wait, utilization, and class-specific wait, and some policies improve one job class while harming another. For example, heuristics such as LRF can reduce large-job delay while worsening small-job delay, whereas WFP3 performs well on wait but may sacrifice utilization. Since the best ordering depends jointly on workload mix, queue state, and optimization target, MCTS benefits more from searching over a diverse heuristic action set than from committing to a single fixed priority rule. This yields a practical branching factor of $16 \times 10 + 1 = 161$ actions, where the additional action is the unmodified FCFS ordering.
